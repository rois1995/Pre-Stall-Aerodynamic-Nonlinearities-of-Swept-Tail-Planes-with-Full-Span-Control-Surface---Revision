\begin{abstract}
\label{anchor:abstract}
On swept wings with deflected trailing-edge controls, pitching moment can change while total lift continues to grow before stall. MONNALISA wind-tunnel measurements and Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes computations are used to examine the integrated response and resolve the computed fixed-surface and full-span-control loads. For the aft-swept reference model, the fixed surface continues gaining lift but accounts for about two-thirds of the total shortfall in lift growth relative to the preceding incidence trend. The control contributes more strongly to the corresponding pitching-moment change about the aerodynamic center. Pressure distributions and regional integrations show stronger leading-edge suction alongside reduced intermediate- and aft-chord loading, with moment contributions also governed by the spanwise locations of these forces. The tested dihedral configuration has lower absolute lift but a larger increment over the examined pre-stall interval, while the forward-swept planform has weaker aft-loading losses concentrated inboard. Despite differences between measured and computed integrated responses, the load decomposition reveals how lift growth and pitching moment can have distinct spatial origins.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A deflected trailing-edge control changes both lift and the location where that load acts. Consequently, the pitching moment can change abruptly even while total lift grows below stall. A local reduction in loading need not coincide with a reduction in integrated lift, because force contributions at different chordwise and spanwise positions combine. Explaining this behavior requires resolving fixed- and moving-surface loads rather than examining integrated coefficients alone.

Plain-flap experiments by Sears and Liddell~\cite{sears1942plain} documented reduced flap effectiveness associated with separation at large deflections. They also showed that similar lift changes can accompany different hinge-moment behavior. On finite rectangular wings with full-span flaps, Campbell et al.~\cite{campbell1956fullspan} related flap chord and aspect ratio to lift and center of pressure. Together, these studies establish the importance of both flap loading and the position of the resulting forces.



On swept wings, spanwise changes in local lift can produce pitching-moment nonlinearity before maximum lift~\cite{harper1964sweptstall}. The onset of local stall need not coincide with the change in integrated moment: the force change and its moment arm vary along the span. This motivates analyzing load redistribution instead of assigning a single global stall event.

Swept-flap experiments reinforce this distinction. Tewes et al.~\cite{tewes2014sweepcontrol} found that large deflection could produce separated flap flow with continued lift growth, while sweep altered the associated lift and pitching-moment response. Melton~\cite{melton2014sweptflap} observed spanwise variation in separated flow on a swept wing with a 30\%-chord flap. These lower-Reynolds-number experiments primarily investigated active separation control and cannot validate the present configurations quantitatively. They establish context for the three-dimensional response but do not resolve the fixed- and control-surface contributions.

Reduced-order methods provide another perspective: Sivells and Neely~\cite{sivells1947liftingline} incorporated nonlinear sectional lift and moment data into lifting-line analysis, while Goitia and Llamas~\cite{goitia2019vlm} combined viscous sectional data with vortex-lattice and finite-wing corrections. Such formulations can predict nonlinear loading and pitching moment, while the present RANS solutions resolve the surface pressure and wall shear used to examine where those loads change.

The MONNALISA database study~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa} documented pre-stall lift nonlinearity, reverse flow on the control, associated pressure changes, and continued total lift growth. Its DUST computations~\cite{tugnoli2021dust} used nonlinear sectional data, while the potential-based calculations were smoother. The present study builds on those observations but addresses a different question: how the loads on the fixed surface and control combine into lift and pitching-moment changes.

An actual increase of fixed-surface lift does not establish that its growth follows the preceding incidence trend; similarly, a control lift loss alone does not establish its contribution to pitching moment. We compare actual increments with those extrapolated from preceding incidence, integrate pressure loads by chord and span, and connect them to skin friction. Integrated WT--RANS comparisons first delimit the interpretation of computed surface loads. The two dihedral orientations and the opposite-sweep planform then reveal how load magnitudes and moment arms influence the response.

The models are isolated tailplane-inspired wings with full-span, 30\%-chord plain controls, without fuselage or wing wake. The results do not establish behavior for partial-span controls or untested geometries.

\label{end:sec:intro}
\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Models and wind-tunnel setup}\label{sec:setup}
Wind-tunnel (WT) measurements provide integrated lift and moment; RANS surface loads resolve their component contributions. Configuration I is aft swept, with $\Gamma=0\degc$ and $45\degc$; configuration II is forward swept and has a different overall planform.

Both wings use a constant symmetric section with thickness ratio $t/c=9\%$, geometric semispan $b=1.6$~m, and a full-span plain control hinged at 70\% chord. Coefficients use the reference semiplanform area $S$ and mean aerodynamic chord $\bar c$, independent of dihedral rotation. Table~\ref{tab:geometry} lists the geometry: quarter-chord sweep $\lambda=+30\degc$ for configuration I and $-15\degc$ for II. Positive $\Gamma$ raises the tip; aspect ratio for the full wing is $\Lambda=2b^2/S$. Configuration II also differs in taper, aspect ratio, area, and chord, so the response cannot be attributed to sweep alone.

\subsection{Conventions and moment references}\label{sec:references}
Positive incidence raises the leading edge, positive control deflection moves the trailing edge downward, and positive pitching moment is nose-up. Body axes are downstream ($x$), spanwise ($y$) and upward ($z$). Forces are normalized by $q_\infty S$; lift and drag follow from the body-axis components:
\begin{equation}
 C_L=C_{F,z}\cos\alpha-C_{F,x}\sin\alpha,\qquad
 C_D=C_{F,z}\sin\alpha+C_{F,x}\cos\alpha,\qquad
 C_{F,k}=F_k/(q_\infty S).
\label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
Pitching moment is normalized by $q_\infty S\bar c$. Body coordinates are expressed relative to the root leading edge.

\label{anchor:fitted_reference}Experimental and numerical aerodynamic centers are fitted separately from zero-deflection moment and body-normal-force trends over $|\alpha|\leq10\degc$ and then fixed as control deflection changes~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa}. For configuration I, WT/RANS positions are $0.6430/0.6420$~m at zero dihedral and $0.6455/0.6435$~m at $45\degc$, differences of about 1 and 2~mm. For II, the positions are $-0.0492/-0.00362$~m, differing by 45.6~mm. Thus matching the reference construction does not impose identical moment axes on WT and RANS; the common-axis dihedral diagnostic below explicitly uses one physical reference.

The original numerical reference is $O=(0.228571,0,0)$~m, the root quarter-chord of Geom.~I. For a numerical pole transported from abscissa $x_0$ to $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_1)=C_M(x_0)+\frac{x_1-x_0}{\bar c}C_{F,z}.
\label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:geometry} lists the fitted abscissae. Matched dihedral diagnostics use the identical numerical axis $A_I=(0.642000,0,0)$~m in both configurations, denoted $C_M^{A_I}$. Geom.~II uses its own MAC once for every moment contribution; all coefficients use the fixed areas in Table~\ref{tab:geometry}.

\label{end:sec:references}
\subsection{Numerical setup}\label{sec:numerics}
SU2~\cite{palacios2013stanford} solves compressible ideal-gas RANS with Sutherland viscosity, Spalart--Allmaras turbulence~\cite{spalart1992sa}, and the Bas--Cakmakcioglu transition model~\cite{cakmakcioglu2020bcm}. Vertex-centered median-dual cells use Roe fluxes, second-order MUSCL reconstruction~\cite{van1979towards}, Green--Gauss gradients and a Venkatakrishnan limiter~\cite{venkatakrishnan1993accuracy} (coefficient 0.05); turbulence convection uses first-order upwinding.

\label{anchor:wall_model}The wing has no-slip, adiabatic walls, freestream far-field and root symmetry boundaries. Steady pseudo-time iteration uses implicit Euler, FGMRES and adaptive CFL. The lift/drag Cauchy stopping tolerance is approximately $3\times10^{-6}$ over 100 entries.

Hex-dominant, octree-based meshes~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa} resolve the leading edge, hinge junction, trailing edge, and incidence-dependent wake. Boundary-layer extrusion has nominal first-cell height $2\times10^{-6}$~m and growth ratio 1.15. Representative median wall distance is $y^+=0.19$--0.26, with maxima 1.1--1.3.

A four-level grid study on configuration I used $\delta=35\degc$, $\Gamma=0,45\degc$ and $\alpha=0,18\degc$ (Table~\ref{tab:mesh}, Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh}). Nominal meshes had 9.7--12.7 million points versus 50.1--65.9 million on the finest level. Moments in Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh} use the original numerical pole.

Nominal-to-finest differences were 1.20--2.14\% in lift and 1.13--2.49\% in drag. At $\Gamma=0\degc$, $\alpha=18\degc$, $C_M^O$ changed from $-0.813$ to $-0.827$; the $45\degc$ moment sequence was nonmonotonic. The nominal mesh reduced campaign cost but gives no discretization-error bound for local component loads.

\subsection{Load decomposition and trend departures}\label{sec:analysis}
An actual coefficient increment is its change over a sampled incidence interval. Its departure subtracts the increment predicted by continuing an empirically fitted preceding slope over the same interval. At $\delta=20\degc$, $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, for example, fixed-surface lift increases by $+0.0593$ rather than the predicted $+0.1285$: its departure is $-0.0692$. A negative departure therefore means reduced growth relative to the chosen reference, not necessarily an actual loss of lift.

We integrate pressure and viscous loads over fixed, control and tip surfaces using consistent reference quantities. The control includes the hinge junction and blunt trailing edge; tip loads remain in total coefficients.

For lift or moment coefficient $Q$, the actual increment and departure of component $j$ are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta C_{Q,j}=C_{Q,j}(\alpha_b)-C_{Q,j}(\alpha_a),\qquad
 D_{Q,j}=\Delta C_{Q,j}-m_{Q,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}(\alpha_b-\alpha_a).
\label{eq:departure}
\end{equation}
Each preceding slope is an unweighted least-squares fit using identical incidence samples for all load components, preserving additivity. For $\delta=20\degc$ the primary preceding range is $\alpha\in\{-6,-4,-2\}\degc$; for the early $\delta=30\degc$ feature it is $\{-16,-14,-12\}\degc$. These empirical slopes are not assumed to represent attached flow. Neighboring fitting windows quantify reference sensitivity, while finite differences remain meaningful when the preceding trend is curved.

Propagating the supplied measured-coefficient uncertainties through $D=\sum_i w_i C_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
 u_{\mathrm{ind}}=\left[\sum_i(w_i u_i)^2\right]^{1/2},\qquad
 u_{\mathrm{L1}}=\sum_i|w_i|u_i.
\label{eq:uncertainty}
\end{equation}
The first assumes independent errors. The second bounds the combined perturbation if each sample error is bounded by its supplied magnitude; unknown inter-incidence covariance and coverage prevent interpreting these sensitivities as confidence intervals.

Pressure and wall-shear forces are integrated on the triangulated surface over chordwise and spanwise regions. The pressure coefficient is $C_p=(p-p_\infty)/q_\infty$ and wall-shear coefficient $\mathbf C_f=\boldsymbol\tau_w/q_\infty$. The moment about reference abscissa $x_A$ is
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_A)=\frac{1}{q_\infty S\bar c}
 \int\big[z\,dF_x-(x-x_A)dF_z\big].
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
The terms $z\,dF_x$ and $-(x-x_A)dF_z$ are retained separately for common-axis dihedral comparisons. Integrated force and moment reconstructions close against solver totals.

Chordwise regions are forward ($\xi<0.4$), intermediate ($0.4\leq\xi<0.7$), and aft ($\xi\geq0.7$), with $\xi$ the local chord fraction after straightening the control about its hinge. The aft region is further divided into spanwise thirds, using $\eta=s/b$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}). Integrals cover both physical sides, with hinge junction and end-face loads included in the appropriate totals. Pressure cuts at $\eta=0.2,0.5,0.8$ follow the actual surface intersections without smoothing or bridging gaps; the reported regional coefficients are integrated loads, not densities.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.72\textwidth]{figures_revision_load_regions.pdf}
\caption{Reference planform: chordwise integration regions, spanwise thirds, hinge, and pressure-section locations.}\label{fig:regions}\end{figure}

\label{anchor:reversal_definition}For each surface face with unit normal $\mathbf n$, the downstream local chord direction $\mathbf d_c$ is rotated with the control, then projected into the tangent plane as $\mathbf t_c=[\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n]/\|\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n\|$. Thus $C_{f,c}=\mathbf C_f\cdot\mathbf t_c$ measures the chordwise component of wall shear in the local, dihedral- and flap-aware orientation. End faces are excluded from this shear diagnostic.

The fraction $f_{\mathrm{rev}}$ is the upper-control area fraction ($\xi>0.7$, excluding end faces) with $C_{f,c}<0$. A value of 0.5 means that upstream-directed chordwise shear occupies half the control's upper surface. It indicates near-wall reversal, not a complete three-dimensional separation footprint~\cite{tobak1982topology,mccormack1949sweptforward}.

\subsection{Pressure distribution and pitching moment}\label{sec:pressure}
Figure~\ref{fig:cp} compares pressure at $\alpha=0\degc$ and $2\degc$ on both sides of the reference wing at three span stations. Leading-edge suction strengthens, while the upper-side suction minimum near the hinge becomes weaker. Here, pressure recovery means the chordwise rise in pressure downstream of that hinge-region minimum. The increased leading-edge suction alone might suggest stronger fixed-surface loading, but it does not establish how the *integrated* lift growth changes: suction can strengthen near the leading edge while loading elsewhere on the fixed chord stagnates or decreases. The subsequent regional integrals test those competing contributions, rather than inferring a net result from isolated pressure peaks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_pressure_sections.pdf}
\caption{Chordwise pressure distributions at three span locations across the reference pre-stall loading change.}\label{fig:cp}\end{figure}

Integrated over the semispan, the forward chord ($\xi<0.4$) gains $+0.0671$ in lift coefficient, but less than the expected $+0.1027$ from its preceding trend; its departure is $-0.0357$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}a). The intermediate fixed chord instead loses $-0.0097$ rather than gaining $+0.0257$, departing by $-0.0354$. 

The aft region loses $-0.0332$, compared with an expected $+0.0063$, giving a departure of $-0.0396$. Pressure supplies nearly all the principal lift departure, while viscous forces remain included in the integration.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_spatial_changes.pdf}
\caption{Chordwise and spanwise contributions to reference lift and pitching-moment changes over the selected pre-stall interval.}\label{fig:spatial}\end{figure}

Across the inboard, middle, and outboard thirds of the aft chord, actual lift increments are similar ($-0.0103,-0.0118,-0.0111$), but corresponding moment increments grow ($+0.00252,+0.00612,+0.00910$; Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}b,c). The aft-swept planform places outboard aft loading farther downstream of the aerodynamic center. Its longer $x-x_A$ arm makes comparable losses of upward body-normal force produce greater nose-up moment changes through $-(x-x_A)dF_z$; the included $z\,dF_x$ term is small in this reference case. These are actual increments between computed states, not departures from the extrapolated baseline used in the chordwise shortfall comparison. Hence the chordwise and spanwise analyses explain complementary aspects of the response: the former identifies where the unexpected reduction in lift growth occurs, and the latter why the resulting force change carries different pitching-moment weight along the span.

\subsection{Skin friction and reverse flow}\label{sec:reversal}
Figure~\ref{fig:reversal} tracks the upper-control area fraction with upstream-directed chordwise shear; the open diamond marks the $\alpha=0\degc$ reference state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.90\textwidth]{figures_revision_reversal.pdf}
\caption{Reference zero-dihedral upper control surface area fraction with reverse flow at $\delta=20\degc$ and $\delta=30\degc$.}\label{fig:reversal}\end{figure}

At $\delta=20\degc$, this fraction rises from 0.146 to 0.485 across $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, coinciding with the computed lift-growth shortfall and positive moment change. Figure~\ref{fig:transition} shows that the reversed-shear region also changes location.

At $\alpha=0\degc$, reverse chordwise shear lies chiefly near the hinge; at $2\degc$ it is concentrated toward the trailing edge, and by $4\degc$ extends upstream (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}). Thus the increase in the upper-control reversed-shear area across the key interval does not represent uniform growth of the original hinge-adjacent zone. This evolving near-wall pattern accompanies weakened hinge suction and reduced aft loading in the pressure and force integrals. Its spatial development is informative even though the sign of a single wall-shear component cannot uniquely identify the full three-dimensional separation topology.

At $4\degc$, the reversed-shear fraction reaches about 59\%, even though total lift grows more over $\alpha=2\to4\degc$ ($+0.0968$) than over $0\to2\degc$ ($+0.0242$). A larger reversal area need not imply a smaller total lift increment.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_surface_sequence.pdf}
\caption{Reference upper-surface chordwise skin friction across the loading changes at $\delta=20\degc$ (top) and $\delta=30\degc$ (bottom) deflection.}\label{fig:transition}\end{figure}

At $\delta=30\degc$, the corresponding loading change occurs earlier, over $\alpha=-10\to-8\degc$: fixed-surface lift grows by $+0.0406$ while the control loses $-0.0436$, giving a small total decrease ($-0.0030$). The reversed-shear fraction rises from 0.622 to 0.935 over this interval (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}, lower row).

Unlike the reinforcing moment departures at $\delta=20\degc$, those at $30\degc$ partly cancel: the fixed-surface contribution is negative and the control contribution positive. The total moment departure ranges from $-0.0262$ to $+0.0076$ across preceding-slope fits, so its sign is not robust. Taken together, the two deflections show why reversed-shear area alone cannot determine integrated lift or moment.

\subsection{Effect of dihedral}\label{sec:dihedral}
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_dihedral} compares the dihedral WT polars and the $\Gamma=45\degc$ WT--RANS response. At $\delta=20\degc$, measured lift growth remains suppressed over $\alpha=-2\to2\degc$ while the moment departure over $-2\to0\degc$ is below both uncertainty sensitivities (Table~\ref{tab:wt_geometry}). At $\delta=30\degc$, lift growth decreases over $-2\to0\degc$, but moment falls earlier ($-0.027$ over $-4\to-2\degc$); its departure does not exceed the conservative uncertainty sensitivity.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_comparison_dihedral.pdf}
\caption{Wind-tunnel dihedral and positive-dihedral WT--RANS comparisons. WT bars show propagated pointwise uncertainties; plain and hatched bands mark selected incidence intervals.}\label{fig:comparison_dihedral}\end{figure}

RANS corresponds less closely to the dihedral measurements. At $\delta=20\degc$ it predicts a lift decrease over $\alpha=-6\to-4\degc$ absent from WT; that computed interval also has a marked increase in the control reversed-shear fraction. Near the measured feature, by contrast, successive numerical lift increments decrease from 0.0763 to 0.0444 over $-2\to0\degc$ and $0\to2\degc$, but the measured weak moment response is not reproduced. At $\delta=30\degc$, the computed moment decrease occurs one incidence interval later than measured. The following decomposition examines how those computed dihedral loads change and contribute to pitching moment.

At $\alpha=0\degc$, $\delta=20\degc$, the computed $45\degc$-dihedral lift is 0.301 lower than at zero dihedral. Projecting the zero-dihedral panel-normal loading onto the inclined lift direction, without changing its magnitude, accounts algebraically for about 0.247 of the difference. The incidence-dependent response is examined separately below.

The computed ordering of lift increments reverses with incidence: at $\delta=20\degc$, lift grows by 0.1210 ($\Gamma=0\degc$) versus 0.0763 ($45\degc$) over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, but by 0.0242 versus 0.0444 over $0\to2\degc$. Thus dihedral does more than shift absolute lift; the relative *increment* changes with incidence and cannot be described by a fixed projection factor. For the latter interval, the $45\degc$ fixed surface still gains lift while the control loses it (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget}), and all three surface groups contribute to its negative pitching-moment increment about its own aerodynamic center. The zero-dihedral model nevertheless retains the larger absolute lift in both states. Comparing increments isolates a different aspect of the response than comparing the lift levels at one incidence.

\caption{Actual coefficient increments for the dihedral and forward-swept cases; moments are about their corresponding numerical aerodynamic centers.}\label{tab:geometry_budget}
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}\toprule
Geom., $\Gamma$, $\delta$, $\alpha$ interval & Increment & Fixed & Control & Tip & Total \\\midrule

Dihedral elevates the loading above the reference axis, adding an axial-force moment through $z\,dF_x$, while the normal-force contribution uses $-(x-x_A)dF_z$. Because the moment pole affects a comparison of dihedral configurations~\cite{maggin1946dihedral}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dihedral} transports both numerical cases to the same axis $A_I$. Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget} instead uses each configuration's own aerodynamic center; the corresponding component increments must not be subtracted without first accounting for this difference in reference. The surface decomposition identifies where the changed loads act; the common-axis decomposition then separates their axial- and normal-force moment contributions. For a matched incidence interval, the difference of those actual moment increments is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_M=[C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
             -[C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
A negative $\mathcal I_M$ means that the moment increase is smaller, or the decrease larger, with dihedral over the same incidence interval. These are differences of actual moment increments, not departures from preceding fitted trends.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_dihedral_intervals.pdf}
\caption{Dihedral differences in moment increments about the common axis $A_I$ and their $F_x$ and $F_z$ contributions: $\delta=20\degc$ (left) and $\delta=30\degc$ (right).}\label{fig:dihedral}\end{figure}

At $\delta=20\degc$, the force-moment differences partly compensate over $\alpha=-4\to-2\degc$ ($-0.00362$ from $F_x$, $+0.00131$ from $F_z$), but reinforce over $0\to2\degc$ ($-0.01283$ and $-0.02750$, total $\mathcal I_M=-0.04033$). The larger normal-force contribution in the latter interval reflects changes in both loading and its streamwise moment arm, which cannot be inferred from a fixed lift-projection factor (Fig.~\ref{fig:dihedral}).

At $\delta=30\degc$, the axial- and normal-force terms also reinforce over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, producing a moment-increment difference of $-0.01836$. The relative contributions thus reflect the incidence-dependent forces and their moment arms, rather than a constant geometric scaling.

The $\delta=30\degc$ moment decrease occurs while upper-control reversed-shear area changes little: 0.889, 0.911 and 0.912 at $\alpha=-2,0,2\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal_geometry}). Over $-2\to0\degc$, $C_M^A$ decreases by 0.0148, of which $-0.0112$ comes from the fixed surface (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget}). The fixed-surface flow thus warrants separate examination.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.90\textwidth]{figures_revision_reversal_geometry.pdf}
\caption{Reverse-flow area fractions on the upper control surface for $\Gamma=45\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$ and $\delta=30\degc$.}\label{fig:reversal_geometry}\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow_dihedral} shows increased chordwise shear developing from midspan toward the tip over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$. At $2\degc$, a narrow outboard leading-edge region has upstream-directed shear followed downstream by positive shear and a sharp rise. The accompanying pressure sections show localized suction peaks, so the combined signatures are compatible with a local separation-and-reattachment process, possibly associated with a transition-related boundary-layer change. They do not independently establish the position of laminar--turbulent transition, nor the complete topology of a three-dimensional separation bubble. Measurements on a finite unswept wing by Toppings and Yarusevych~\cite{toppings2021wingtip} demonstrate that bubble-related features can vary substantially along the span, but at different Reynolds number and geometry. We use this comparison only as physical context; the present interpretation rests on the computed pressure and signed-shear fields.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_flow_dihedral.pdf}
\caption{$\Gamma=45\degc$, $\delta=30\degc$: upper-surface shear maps (top), upper-surface shear cuts (middle), and pressure sections (bottom) at $\alpha=-2,0,2\degc$.}\label{fig:flow_dihedral}\end{figure}

The pressure cuts show stronger leading-edge and hinge-region suction at midspan and outboard stations than inboard at $\alpha=0\degc$. The larger suction is followed by recovery, giving the outboard boundary layer a different local pressure-gradient history; this is consistent with the spatial variation of the skin-friction increase and subsequent leading-edge reversal. Taper reduces the outboard chord and dimensional leading-edge radius, potentially concentrating acceleration over a shorter physical distance. Spanwise variations in effective inflow, three-dimensional flow and local chord Reynolds number also influence the pressure distribution, so taper alone does not establish the observed cause. Since the two dihedral configurations share this taper, it can contextualize spanwise variation *within* a planform but cannot independently explain changes due to dihedral.

Control-chord pressure varies little over $\alpha=-2\to2\degc$ while fixed-surface pressure and shear change substantially (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow_dihedral}). This agrees with the fixed-surface dominance of the computed moment change despite extensive control reversal.

\subsection{Aft- and Forward-Swept Planforms}\label{sec:planform}
Configuration II has $-15\degc$ quarter-chord sweep versus $+30\degc$ for I, with the same semispan and full-span control. Alongside the sweep reversal, taper, chord, and area differ between these complete planforms (Table~\ref{tab:geometry}).

The wind-tunnel results in Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison_planform} show configuration II's local reduction in lift growth at $\delta=20\degc$ over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$: lift increases by 0.044, compared with approximately 0.092 over both $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$ and $\alpha=2\to4\degc$. Moment increases by 0.019 over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$. This feature occurs later than in configuration I, with a smaller moment rise over its interval. The primary moment departure in Table~\ref{tab:wt_geometry} exceeds both uncertainty sensitivities, although neighboring preceding fitting ranges do not uniformly exceed the primary L1 sensitivity. At $\delta=30\degc$, the reference model's broad early feature does not repeat at the same negative incidences in II.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_comparison_planform.pdf}
\caption{Wind-tunnel planform and forward-swept WT--RANS comparisons. WT bars show propagated pointwise uncertainties; grey bands mark the measured $\delta=20\degc$ interval.}\label{fig:comparison_planform}\end{figure}

At $\delta=20\degc$, the computed lift increase over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ is 0.0935, compared with 0.044 measured. Both WT and RANS show a positive pitching-moment increment. The numerical increment is $+0.0168$ about its own aerodynamic center, or approximately $+0.0099$ after transport to the experimental center using Eq.~\ref{eq:transport} and $\Delta C_{F,z}=0.0952$, compared with $+0.019$ measured. The spatial analysis below examines the computed load redistribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:planform_loading} separates the planform lift and moment increments over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, $\delta=20\degc$. Configuration II has a larger fixed-surface lift gain ($+0.1014$ versus $+0.0593$) and smaller control loss ($-0.0079$ versus $-0.0351$), yielding a larger total lift increment ($+0.0935$ versus $+0.0242$) but smaller moment increment ($+0.0168$ versus $+0.0339$). Along the chord, II gains lift in the intermediate region ($+0.0063$), where I loses it ($-0.0097$), and has much smaller aft losses ($-0.0065$ versus $-0.0332$). The forward region gains lift on both models, but the differing intermediate and aft increments distinguish their redistribution. These are per-configuration normalized coefficient increments about their respective aerodynamic centers, so the comparison is of the complete planform responses rather than a force difference at one common moment pole.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_planform_loading.pdf}
\caption{Planform comparison over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, $\delta=20\degc$: lift increments (top) and aerodynamic-center moment increments (bottom), with matching decompositions.}\label{fig:planform_loading}\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!tp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.50\textwidth]{figures_revision_reversal_planform.pdf}
\caption{Forward-swept upper control surface reverse-flow area fraction at $\delta=20\degc$; the open diamond marks $\alpha=0\degc$, the start of the interval in the loading analysis.}\label{fig:reversal_planform}\end{figure}

The aft loading also shifts spanwise. On I, comparable aft lift losses occur across the span, but the positive moment contribution increases outboard as the downstream arm grows with aft sweep. On II, aft loss and positive moment change are concentrated inboard, while the outboard aft region gains lift; the downstream arm instead decreases toward the tip. The factor $-(x-x_A)dF_z$ therefore gives a different spanwise weighting of the force changes and helps explain why II's larger total lift increase does not produce a larger moment increment. The contributions also depend on the actual local force distribution, not on a moment-arm factor applied to a uniform span load. Earlier forward-swept-wing observations by McCormack and Cook~\cite{mccormack1949sweptforward} link inboard pressure redistribution to pitching moment in a wing without controls; that prior result is context rather than a directly matching loading mechanism.

Despite its larger actual lift increment, II also falls below the preceding trend: its fixed/control departures are $-0.0263/-0.0104$, against $-0.0692/-0.0414$ on I. Moment departures about the respective aerodynamic centers reinforce in both cases, but the II contributions partly cancel about its original numerical origin ($-0.0023/+0.0035$).

On II, the upper-control reversed-shear fraction rises from about 19\% to 47\% and 59\% at $\alpha=0,2,4\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal_planform}). The surface maps in Fig.~\ref{fig:flow_planform} locate the growth chiefly around midspan and then increasingly toward the root, with upstream spreading along the control chord. Over the same 0--2-degree interval, the pressure cuts show weakened inboard hinge suction and comparatively little change at the outboard section, consistent with the inboard concentration of aft lift loss. These observations connect the chordwise and spanwise load integrals to the evolving near-wall signature without assuming that its area fraction alone determines the force.

At $2\degc$, II and I have similar reversed-shear fractions (about 47\% and 49\%) but markedly different control lift losses. Their pressure distributions and load locations—not reversed-shear area alone—account for the different integrated responses.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The wind-tunnel measurements show that pre-stall lift-growth reductions and pitching-moment changes need not coincide in incidence or control deflection. Decomposing the computed loads identifies the different contributions of the fixed surface and the full-span control. For the aft-swept, zero-dihedral model at $\delta=20\degc$, total lift still increases over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, but the fixed surface accounts for about 63\% of the shortfall relative to the preceding lift-growth trend; the control produces the larger pitching-moment departure about the numerical aerodynamic center.

Stronger leading-edge suction accompanies reduced growth over the forward chord and actual lift loss over the intermediate chord. Comparable aft lift losses yield progressively larger positive moment increments toward the tip because the aft-swept moment arms increase outboard. Changes in control-surface reverse flow accompany these load changes, but reversal area alone cannot predict integrated lift or moment.

With $45\degc$ dihedral, adjacent lift increments reverse their ordering relative to the zero-dihedral model, while the axial- and normal-force moment contributions alternately reinforce and partly compensate about a common reference axis. The forward-swept planform exhibits greater fixed-surface lift growth, weaker control losses and an inboard-concentrated aft-loading change. These comparisons show that the magnitude and spatial distribution of load changes both shape the pitching-moment response.

Finer-grid studies can further assess the pre-stall component moments. The present load decomposition shows why continuing total lift growth does not, by itself, reveal the location of the force changes governing pitching moment.
