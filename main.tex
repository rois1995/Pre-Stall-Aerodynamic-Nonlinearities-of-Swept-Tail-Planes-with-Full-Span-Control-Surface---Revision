\begin{abstract}
\label{anchor:abstract}
Local loading changes on a swept lifting surface with a deflected plain control can alter lift growth and pitching moment well before maximum lift. MONNALISA wind-tunnel measurements and Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes calculations are used to examine how fixed-surface and full-span control loads combine in this response. For the positive-sweep, zero-dihedral model with a 20$\degc$ control deflection, the computed fixed surface continues gaining lift while both surface contributions fall below their preceding incidence trends. Across the tested fitting ranges, the fixed surface accounts for about 63\% of the total lift departure; the control contributes more strongly to the moment departure about the fitted aerodynamic center. Pressure sections and regional integrations connect these different component contributions to stronger leading-edge suction, reduced hinge/aft loading, and longer outboard moment arms. Matched dihedral comparisons resolve incidence-dependent changes in lift growth and the force contributions to pitching moment. The forward-swept planform gives weaker aft losses and a different spanwise distribution of moment contributions. The detailed interpretation concerns the computed flow fields and loads; agreement with measured integrated coefficients is partial. The combined force and moment analysis identifies loading changes that continuing total lift growth alone would conceal.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A deflected trailing-edge control changes both the amount of lift generated by a lifting surface and the location at which that load acts. Its response with incidence is therefore relevant to pitching moment as well as lift, even before the maximum-lift condition is reached. When local loading changes, total lift can continue increasing while its growth slows and the pitching response changes more abruptly. Interpreting this behavior requires relating the integrated coefficients to the loads on the fixed and moving parts of the surface.

Two-dimensional experiments on airfoils with plain flaps establish the starting point for this problem. Sears and Liddell~\cite{sears1942plain} measured lift, drag, pitching moment, and hinge moment for a 30\%-chord plain flap on an NACA 0015 airfoil, including sealed and unsealed hinge gaps. Separation reduced flap effectiveness at large deflections, although additional deflection still increased lift within the tested range. Their comparison with a thinner symmetric section also showed that similar lift effectiveness could coexist with different hinge-moment behavior. Reduced flap effectiveness is thus established physics; neither separation nor a single integrated lift trend fully characterizes the loading response.

On a finite wing, flap-induced loading is also shaped by the spanwise flow and planform. Campbell, Blanks, and Leaver~\cite{campbell1956fullspan} investigated rectangular wings of small aspect ratio with full-span plain flaps of several chord fractions. Their force and pitching-moment measurements, compared with thin-wing theory and empirical thickness and boundary-layer allowances, related flap effectiveness and center of pressure to flap chord and aspect ratio. Those unswept, thinner, transition-tripped models differ from the present swept models, but they establish why the location of the additional load matters alongside its magnitude.

For swept wings, Harper and Maki~\cite{harper1964sweptstall} documented how spanwise changes in local lift response can produce pitching-moment nonlinearity below maximum lift. The first appearance of local stall need not coincide with the first integrated moment change, because the force change and its moment arm vary along the span. Their broad stall review provides context for examining load redistribution, rather than an equivalent test of the present deflected-control configurations.

Swept-flap experiments combining pressure, load, and flow-field measurements show that this spanwise organization extends to the flap flow itself. On a flapped NACA 0012 wing with adjustable sweep, Tewes et al.~\cite{tewes2014sweepcontrol} found in the unactuated unswept configuration that the lift slope remained close to its thin-airfoil value although the flow over the deflected flap was separated, whereas the pitching moment increased as the separation location on the flap moved upstream. At 45$\degc$ sweep and zero incidence, the unactuated wing with a large flap deflection produced about half the lift of the unswept wing, and its pitching moment increased almost linearly with incidence instead of remaining constant. On a 30$\degc$ swept NACA 0015 wing with a 30\%-chord flap, Melton~\cite{melton2014sweptflap} reported baseline pressure plateaus on the deflected flap, a large recirculation region near midspan with spanwise velocity directed toward the tip, and a smaller inboard recirculation region. These lower-Reynolds-number studies primarily address active separation control and do not provide quantitative comparisons for the present models. Their unactuated results nevertheless show that flap separation can accompany continued lift growth while altering pitching moment, and that its extent varies along a swept span. They do not resolve how the fixed surface and flap separately contribute to those integrated changes.

Nonlinear sectional data provide one route for representing these responses in reduced-order models. Sivells and Neely~\cite{sivells1947liftingline} coupled such lift data to lifting-line theory and also calculated wing pitching moment from sectional moment data. Goitia and Llamas~\cite{goitia2019vlm} coupled a vortex-lattice formulation to viscous sectional data, with effective-sweep and finite-wing corrections, to predict nonlinear wing and tail lift. Consequently, a reduced-order model's ability to reproduce the response depends on how it represents sectional nonlinearities and changes in three-dimensional loading.

The immediate basis for the present work is the MONNALISA database publication~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa}. Sec.~IV and Fig.~11 already document the baseline pre-stall lift nonlinearity, the expansion of reverse flow on the control, the associated pressure changes, and the continued growth of total lift. Its computations with the DUST mid-fidelity solver of Tugnoli et al.~\cite{tugnoli2021dust} use sectional tables and corrections, while the retained potential-based panel and vortex-lattice predictions are smoother. The present loading analysis builds on these established observations.

The remaining loading question is how changes on the fixed surface and control combine to produce the joint lift and moment response. Continued fixed-surface lift growth, for example, does not establish that this contribution follows its preceding trend. Comparing actual increments with departures from that trend identifies which components supply the reduced total growth, while spatial pressure integrations show why the relative component contributions can differ for moment. This study develops that quantitative interpretation from the existing Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) solutions, first assessing their integrated coefficients against the measured response and its supplied uncertainty. Changes in control deflection and geometry then test how broadly the reference loading pattern persists within the dataset.

The test articles are isolated tailplane-inspired wings with full-span, 30\%-chord plain controls; no fuselage, wing wake, or complete-aircraft trim condition is represented. Dihedral is varied on the same planform, while a forward-swept planform provides the opposite sweep direction. These comparisons define the scope of the analysis but do not establish behavior for partial-span controls, other flap chords, or intermediate dihedral angles.

\label{end:sec:intro}
\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Models and wind-tunnel setup}\label{sec:setup}
Wind-tunnel (WT) measurements establish the integrated lift and pitching-moment response, while RANS surface loads identify the contributions of the fixed surface and control. To examine dihedral, we investigate the reference aft-swept planform (configuration I) at $\Gamma=0\degc$ and $\Gamma=45\degc$, with its in-plane geometry unchanged. A forward-swept planform (configuration II) provides the opposite sweep direction and a different overall planform response. The tables use labels I and II.

Both models use a symmetric section of constant profile along the undeformed semispan $b=1.6$~m, with thickness ratio $t/c=9\%$, where $c$ is the local chord. Each model has a full-span plain control hinged at 70\% chord. The reference surface $S$ is the undeformed semiplanform area, and $\bar c$ denotes its mean aerodynamic chord (MAC). Table~\ref{tab:geometry} gives the geometric parameters and coefficient references. The quarter-chord sweep is $\lambda=+30\degc$ for configuration I and $\lambda=-15\degc$ for configuration II; positive dihedral $\Gamma$ raises the tip. We define aspect ratio for the corresponding full planform as $\Lambda=2b^2/S$, and $\tau$ as the tip-to-root chord ratio. Configuration II also differs in taper, aspect ratio, MAC, and chord-based Reynolds number, so the sweep comparison is interpreted through the overall planform response.

\subsection{Conventions and moment references}\label{sec:references}
Positive incidence $\alpha$ raises the leading edge relative to the freestream, and positive control deflection $\delta$ moves the trailing edge downward relative to the fixed section. Lift is positive toward the upper surface and pitching moment is positive nose-up. The WT and numerical data are expressed in these common physical conventions. In body axes, $x$ is downstream, $y$ is spanwise, and $z$ is upward. The axial force is the downstream body-axis component $F_x$, and the body-normal force is the upward component $F_z$. We define the latter in the common body frame, rather than normal to a panel tilted by dihedral. The body-force components $F_k$, with $k=x,y,z$, are normalized by the freestream dynamic pressure $q_\infty=\rho_\infty U_\infty^2/2$ and area $S$, where $\rho_\infty$ and $U_\infty$ are freestream density and speed. Lift and drag coefficients follow from
\begin{equation}
 C_L=C_{F,z}\cos\alpha-C_{F,x}\sin\alpha,\qquad
 C_D=C_{F,z}\sin\alpha+C_{F,x}\cos\alpha,\qquad
 C_{F,k}=F_k/(q_\infty S).
\label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
Pitching moment is normalized by $q_\infty S\bar c$. Body coordinates are expressed relative to the root leading edge.

\label{anchor:fitted_reference}Both experimental and numerical moments are referred to aerodynamic centers determined from the zero-deflection loading characteristics. Linear fits to moment and body-normal force over $|\alpha|\leq10\degc$ define the positions, which remain fixed when control deflection varies~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa}. The experimental fits use the supplied uncertainty weights; the numerical fits use the retained zero-deflection linear trends. For configuration I, the WT and RANS positions are 0.6430 and 0.6420~m at zero dihedral, and 0.6455 and 0.6435~m at $\Gamma=45\degc$: separations of about 1.0 and 2.0~mm. For configuration II, they are $-0.0492$ and $-0.00362$~m, separated by 45.6~mm. The comparison coefficient $\CM$ therefore uses the same reference construction, with each dataset's fitted position; it is not an imposed quarter-MAC moment.

The original numerical reference is $O=(0.228571,0,0)$~m, the root quarter-chord of Geom.~I. For a numerical pole transported from abscissa $x_0$ to $x_1$,
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_1)=C_M(x_0)+\frac{x_1-x_0}{\bar c}C_{F,z}.
\label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:geometry} lists the fitted abscissae. Matched dihedral diagnostics use the identical numerical axis $A_I=(0.642000,0,0)$~m in both configurations, denoted $C_M^{A_I}$. Geom.~II uses its own MAC once for every moment contribution; all coefficients use the fixed areas in Table~\ref{tab:geometry}.

\label{end:sec:references}
\subsection{Numerical setup}\label{sec:numerics}
The SU2~\cite{palacios2013stanford} calculations solve compressible ideal-gas RANS with Sutherland viscosity, the Spalart--Allmaras (SA) turbulence model~\cite{spalart1992sa}, and the Bas--Cakmakcioglu (BCM) algebraic transition treatment~\cite{cakmakcioglu2020bcm}. The finite-volume mean-flow discretization uses median-dual vertex cells, Roe fluxes, second-order monotonic upstream-centered schemes for conservation laws (MUSCL) reconstruction~\cite{van1979towards}, Green--Gauss gradients, and a Venkatakrishnan limiter~\cite{venkatakrishnan1993accuracy} with coefficient 0.05. SA convection uses first-order scalar upwinding.

\label{anchor:wall_model}The model surfaces have no-slip, adiabatic boundaries, with freestream conditions at the far field and symmetry at the root. The symmetry plane omits the physical support and boundary-layer development over the raised-floor arrangement. Steady solutions use implicit Euler pseudo-time advancement and flexible generalized minimal residual (FGMRES) linear solves, with an adaptive Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) number. The production stopping criterion monitors lift and drag over 100 entries with a Cauchy tolerance of approximately $3\times10^{-6}$.

The surface grid resolves the leading edge, hinge junction, and trailing edge, where curvature and pressure changes require additional resolution. Wall-normal boundary-layer extrusion uses a nominal first-cell height of about $2\times10^{-6}$~m and growth ratio 1.15, while volume refinement follows the wake at each incidence. The volume mesh is hex-dominant and octree-based~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa}. Representative solutions have median nondimensional wall distance $y^+=0.19$--0.26, with local maxima about 1.1--1.3. A reference grid contains approximately 9.44 million points and 18.92 million volume elements.

The existing grid-refinement assessment uses four levels---coarse, nominal, fine, and very fine---for configuration I at $\delta=35\degc$, $\Gamma=0,45\degc$, and $\alpha=0,18\degc$. Table~\ref{tab:mesh} gives the point and element ranges. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh} compares lift, drag, and pitching moment against the actual grid-point count on a logarithmic scale; open squares identify the nominal level, and lines connect the retained levels. Moments are about the numerical origin $O$, using the configuration-I area and MAC.

The nominal level balances spatial resolution with the demands of the extensive MONNALISA campaign across geometries, incidences, and control deflections. Its approximately 9.7--12.7 million points compare with 50.1--65.9 million on the finest level. Nominal-to-finest differences are 1.20--2.14\% for lift and 1.13--2.49\% for drag. At zero dihedral and $\alpha=18\degc$, $C_M^O$ changes from approximately $-0.813$ to $-0.827$, an absolute difference of 0.0134; the $\Gamma=45\degc$ moment sequence is nonmonotonic. The assessment supports the nominal grid as a practical production compromise while showing finite, condition-dependent integrated-load sensitivity. It provides no discretization-error bound for local component loads. The comparison with measurements in Sec.~\ref{sec:response} separately assesses reproduction of the principal pre-stall aerodynamic trends.

\subsection{Load decomposition and trend departures}\label{sec:analysis}
A surface can gain lift and still contribute to a reduction in total lift growth. To distinguish these effects, the actual increment is the measured or computed change in the coefficient between two incidences. The expected increment is obtained by linearly extrapolating the empirically fitted preceding slope over the same interval. Their difference, actual minus expected, is the departure. For the fixed surface of configuration I at zero dihedral and $\delta=20\degc$, the actual increase over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ is $+0.0593$, while continuation of the preceding trend predicts $+0.1285$. The departure is $-0.0692$: the fixed surface still gains lift, but gains 0.0692 less than predicted by that trend. This is a reduction in growth, not an actual lift loss.

The analysis resolves these changes into additive surface loads. Solver surface groups define the fixed surface (upper/lower fixed panels), the control (upper/lower control panels, hinge junction, and blunt trailing edge), and tip. Pressure and viscous contributions are retained on both physical sides with the same global $S$ and $\bar c$. Tip loads remain in every total and closure check. All displayed coefficients use their actual magnitude.

For coefficient label $Q=L$ or $M$ and surface contribution $j$ (fixed, control, tip, or total), the actual increment is the change between sampled incidences $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_b$. Its departure subtracts the change predicted by continuing the preceding slope $m_{Q,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}$, measured per degree,
\begin{equation}
 \Delta C_{Q,j}=C_{Q,j}(\alpha_b)-C_{Q,j}(\alpha_a),\qquad
 D_{Q,j}=\Delta C_{Q,j}-m_{Q,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}(\alpha_b-\alpha_a).
\label{eq:departure}
\end{equation}
The preceding slope is an unweighted linear least-squares fit to the specified preceding incidence samples. Using identical samples and weights for every component and the total preserves additivity for both increments and departures. The primary preceding-incidence range for $\delta=20\degc$ is $\alpha\in\{-6,-4,-2\}\degc$; the early reference $\delta=30\degc$ numerical baseline is $\alpha\in\{-16,-14,-12\}\degc$. These are empirical references, with no assumption that the preceding flow is attached. Neighboring preceding-incidence fitting ranges test this reference dependence, with the same surface solutions. Where the preceding sequence is curved, signed finite differences describe the response without imposing a unique event amplitude.

We evaluate the uncertainty sensitivity of each measured lift or moment departure by propagating the supplied coefficient uncertainties through its defining linear combination. Equation~\ref{eq:departure} is written as $D=\sum_i w_iC_i$, where $i$ indexes the measured samples, $w_i$ is the combined coefficient weight, and $u_i$ is its supplied propagated uncertainty magnitude. The independence-based sensitivity $u_{\mathrm{ind}}$ and absolute-weight sensitivity $u_{\mathrm{L1}}$ are
\begin{equation}
 u_{\mathrm{ind}}=\left[\sum_i(w_i u_i)^2\right]^{1/2},\qquad
 u_{\mathrm{L1}}=\sum_i|w_i|u_i.
\label{eq:uncertainty}
\end{equation}
The first is conditional on independence; the second bounds a perturbation when each sample varies within its supplied magnitude. Coverage and between-incidence covariance are undocumented, so these quantify sensitivity to the supplied uncertainties rather than confidence intervals.

To locate the changes identified by the component load decomposition, we integrate pressure and wall-shear forces over chordwise and spanwise regions. The pressure coefficient is $C_p=(p-p_\infty)/q_\infty$, and the skin-friction vector is $\mathbf C_f=\boldsymbol\tau_w/q_\infty$, where $p_\infty$ is freestream pressure and $\boldsymbol\tau_w$ is wall shear. Linear surface tractions and their moments are integrated over the triangulated surface, with
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_A)=\frac{1}{q_\infty S\bar c}
 \int\big[z\,dF_x-(x-x_A)dF_z\big].
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
Here $x_A$ is the moment-reference abscissa. The moment of the local downstream force $dF_x$ is $z\,dF_x$, and that of the local upward force $dF_z$ is $-(x-x_A)dF_z$. These $F_x$ and $F_z$ contributions are retained separately for the common-axis dihedral comparison. Reconstructed full-surface forces and moments agree with the solver-integrated coefficients before decomposition into chordwise and spanwise load contributions; detailed closure checks are retained in the reproducibility records.

The regions distinguish loading upstream of the hinge from loading on the deflected control, and compare inboard, middle, and outboard contributions. The nondimensional span position is $\eta=s/b$, where $s$ is distance along the undeformed span; $\xi$ is the local chord fraction after straightening the control about its hinge. Chordwise regions are forward ($\xi<0.4$), intermediate ($0.4\leq\xi<0.7$), and aft ($\xi\geq0.7$). Chordwise contributions are summed over the complete semispan, while the aft spanwise contributions use semispan thirds (Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}). Contributions include both physical sides, with end-face loads completing the total. Sectional $C_p$ is shown at $\eta=0.2,0.5,0.8$ on both physical sides. The reference sections use the fixed and control panels, excluding narrow hinge-junction and end faces; the dihedral and forward-swept sections use complete surface intersections, including the junction and blunt trailing edge. Curves follow the connected surface intersections without bridging gaps or smoothing. Regional contributions are integrated loads, rather than load densities.

\caption{Undeformed reference planform: chordwise integration regions, spanwise thirds, hinge, and pressure-section locations.}\label{fig:regions}\end{figure}

\label{anchor:reversal_definition}The local downstream direction must account for control rotation and surface orientation before reverse flow is assessed. Let $\mathbf d_c$ be the downstream chord direction: $(1,0,0)$ on the fixed panel, rotated about the physical hinge on the control. On each surface triangle with unit normal $\mathbf n$, its tangent-plane projection is normalized to give $\mathbf t_c=[\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n]/\|\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n\|$. This construction points downstream on both physical sides, includes dihedral and flap rotation, and is applied after transforming symmetry-reflected states into the common physical convention. End faces are excluded from the reverse-flow area calculation; the retained upper-control faces have no degenerate projected directions.

The signed coefficient $C_{f,c}=\mathbf C_f\cdot\mathbf t_c$ is negative where the chordwise component of wall shear points upstream, indicating local reverse flow. We report the fraction $f_{\mathrm{rev}}$ of the upper side of the deflected control ($\xi>0.7$, excluding end faces) satisfying $C_{f,c}<0$, weighted by actual surface area. Thus $f_{\mathrm{rev}}=0.5$ means that upstream-directed chordwise shear occupies half that upper-control area. Linear interpolation within each triangle locates the sign crossing. This area fraction measures the extent of reverse flow in the local chordwise direction near the wall. It is not a complete three-dimensional separation footprint: three-dimensional separation is characterized by the topology of the full skin-friction field~\cite{tobak1982topology}, and on swept wings separation need not be accompanied by flow reversal~\cite{mccormack1949sweptforward}. The scalar criterion therefore identifies one physically meaningful near-wall signature, without a topological analysis.

The computed sequence has weaker correspondence with these measurements. At $\delta=20\degc$, numerical lift decreases over $\alpha=-6\to-4\degc$ while measured lift increases; this earlier computed change, with a rise in the upper-control reverse-flow area fraction from 0.12 to 0.54, has no measured counterpart. At $\delta=30\degc$, the negative numerical moment change occurs over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, one sampled interval later than the measured change. The following decomposition consequently concerns the computed dihedral response.

At $\alpha=0\degc$, $\delta=20\degc$, the computed lift coefficient is approximately 0.301 lower with $\Gamma=45\degc$. Projection of the reference panel-normal loading onto the common lift direction accounts algebraically for about 0.247 of this difference; changes in local loading and the spanwise-force contribution supply the remainder. The absolute lift difference therefore includes a substantial projection contribution, while the successive incidence increments require the separate load analysis below.

The computed lift increments change differently with incidence on the two models. At the same $\delta=20\degc$ deflection, the actual lift increase over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$ is 0.1210 at zero dihedral and 0.0763 at $\Gamma=45\degc$. Across the next interval, $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, the ordering reverses: the increases are 0.0242 and 0.0444. Although the dihedral model has lower absolute lift, it gains more lift over that latter interval. The change in ordering shows that the relative lift growth depends on incidence. Its moment about its computed aerodynamic center decreases due to contributions from the fixed surface, control, and tip. Pitching moment depends on where $F_x$ and $F_z$ act. Dihedral places the loading above the reference axis, so $F_x$ contributes through the vertical arm $z$. The $F_z$ contribution is weighted by the downstream arm $x-x_A$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:moment}). Both numerical solutions are referred to the same physical axis $A_I$. Maggin and Shanks~\cite{maggin1946dihedral} showed why this choice matters: for a 40$\degc$ swept-back wing, moments about fixed reference axes indicated an apparent increase in longitudinal stability with geometric dihedral, whereas only a slight change remained when each configuration was referred to its own mean aerodynamic chord. The common axis therefore compares the loading and its moment arms about one physical point, whereas Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget} uses each configuration's computed aerodynamic center. Figure~\ref{fig:dihedral}(a,b) compares their moment increments through
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_M=[C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
             -[C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
A negative $\mathcal I_M$ means that the moment increase is smaller, or the decrease larger, with dihedral over the same incidence interval. These are differences of actual moment increments, not departures from preceding fitted trends.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_dihedral_intervals.pdf}
\caption{Dihedral differences in moment increments about the common axis $A_I$ and their $F_x$ and $F_z$ contributions: $\delta=20\degc$ (left) and $\delta=30\degc$ (right).}\label{fig:dihedral}\end{figure}

At $\delta=20\degc$, the $F_x$ moment term gives a negative difference in every plotted interval. Over $\alpha=-4\to-2\degc$, it contributes $-0.00362$, partly offset by a $+0.00131$ contribution from $F_z$, leaving $\mathcal I_M=-0.00231$. Across $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, the terms instead reinforce: $-0.01283$ from $F_x$ and $-0.02750$ from $F_z$ give $-0.04033$. The $F_z$ moment term supplies the larger difference during this reference loading change. Over $\alpha=2\to4\degc$, its contribution falls to $-0.00260$, while the contribution from $F_x$ remains $-0.01412$ and dominates the $-0.01672$ total. A single relative weighting of the two moment terms therefore cannot describe the sequence.

At $\delta=30\degc$, the terms reinforce over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$: $-0.00808$ from $F_x$ and $-0.01027$ from $F_z$ give a total $-0.01836$. Over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, both remain negative, with the $F_x$ contribution larger. Over $\alpha=2\to4\degc$, a positive difference from $F_z$ of $+0.00556$ offsets part of the $-0.01286$ difference from $F_x$, leaving $-0.00730$. This change from reinforcement to compensation concerns the moment-term differences between geometries; it is distinct from fixed/control moment-departure reinforcement in Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}.

The next step is to establish whether these moment changes accompany expansion of reverse flow on the control or loading changes elsewhere on the model. At $\delta=30\degc$, the upper-control reverse-flow area fraction is already 0.889 at $\alpha=-2\degc$, increasing only to 0.911 and 0.912 at $\alpha=0\degc$ and $\alpha=2\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal_geometry}). Over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, the moment about this configuration's computed aerodynamic center nevertheless decreases by 0.0148, with the fixed surface supplying $-0.0112$ of that increment (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget}). This combination motivates examining the fixed-surface pressure and skin friction over the same incidence sequence.

Figure~\ref{fig:flow_dihedral} relates this extensive reverse flow over the control surface to the changing flow over the fixed surface. The middle row adds upper-surface $C_{f,c}$ cuts at the same span stations as the pressure sections. Between $\alpha=-2\degc$ and $\alpha=0\degc$, a band of increased chordwise shear develops from approximately midspan toward the tip. This abrupt rise in $C_{f,c}$ is transition-like. At $\alpha=2\degc$, a narrow leading-edge region of upstream-directed chordwise shear appears over roughly the outer third of the span. At $\eta=0.8$, this short region of negative shear is followed by recovered downstream shear and a sharp increase in its magnitude. Together with the local pressure distribution, the pattern is compatible with a local separation/reattachment process and could indicate a transition-related change in the computed boundary layer. The surface data do not independently identify the laminar--turbulent transition location or a complete three-dimensional bubble topology. On an unswept finite wing at a much lower Reynolds number, Toppings and Yarusevych~\cite{toppings2021wingtip} measured substantial spanwise variation in separation-bubble length, thickness, and reverse-flow velocity, with an open topology and eventual suppression near the tip. Spanwise variation of such features is therefore plausible on a finite wing, although those measurements do not define the present swept-wing state.

The pressure sections show the accompanying spanwise variation of loading. At $\alpha=0\degc$, the leading-edge suction is weak at $\eta=0.2$, whereas distinct peaks appear at $\eta=0.5$ and $0.8$. Hinge-region suction also varies with span, with stronger peaks at the middle and outboard cuts. Stronger suction followed by pressure recovery gives the outboard boundary layer a different pressure-gradient history, consistent with the shear rise and leading-edge reverse flow appearing there first. Spanwise variation in local inflow may contribute to the suction difference. Taper reduces the local chord and, for the same section shape, the dimensional leading-edge radius toward the tip. The leading-edge acceleration can then occur over a shorter physical distance, increasing the spatial acceleration and pressure gradient under otherwise comparable conditions. This tendency is consistent with the stronger outboard suction, while the computed pressure distribution also reflects the coupled three-dimensional flow and the spanwise change in chord Reynolds number. Both dihedral configurations share this taper, so it contextualizes the spanwise variation within each model but does not explain the difference produced by dihedral.

Over most of the control chord, the pressure curves change little between $\alpha=-2\degc$ and $\alpha=2\degc$, consistent with the nearly unchanged reverse-flow area. The fixed-surface shear and pressure changes therefore occur while the control remains extensively separated. The component increments in Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget} and the $F_x/F_z$ terms in Fig.~\ref{fig:dihedral}(a,b) show how this loading produces the computed moment decrease.

\subsection{Effect of sweep direction}\label{sec:planform}
The second comparison reverses quarter-chord sweep from $\lambda=+30\degc$ to $\lambda=-15\degc$ while retaining the 1.6~m semispan and full-span 30\%-chord control. Sweep is the principal geometric difference. Area and MAC differ by approximately 4\% and 8\%, respectively, and taper changes from 0.4 to 0.6 (Table~\ref{tab:geometry}); these accompanying differences prevent attributing the response to sweep alone.

The wind-tunnel results in Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison_planform} show configuration II's local reduction in lift growth at $\delta=20\degc$ over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$: lift increases by 0.044, compared with approximately 0.092 over both $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$ and $\alpha=2\to4\degc$. Moment increases by 0.019 over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$. This feature occurs later than in configuration I, with a smaller moment rise over its interval. The primary moment departure in Table~\ref{tab:wt_geometry} exceeds both uncertainty sensitivities, although neighboring preceding fitting ranges do not uniformly exceed the primary L1 sensitivity. At $\delta=30\degc$, the reference model's broad early feature does not repeat at the same negative incidences in II.

\caption{Wind-tunnel planform comparison (top) and forward-swept WT--RANS comparison (bottom). Grey band marks the measured $\delta=20\degc$ interval.}\label{fig:comparison_planform}\end{figure}

RANS gives an $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ lift increase of 0.0935 at $\delta=20\degc$, more than twice the measured value. Its moment increase, 0.0168, is locally close to the measured 0.019, although each moment is taken about its own aerodynamic center. This weaker lift correspondence limits the use of its local solution to explain the measured feature.

Figure~\ref{fig:planform_loading} shows how the two planforms redistribute loading over the same $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ interval at $\Gamma=0\degc$, $\delta=20\degc$. The forward-swept fixed surface gains $+0.1014$ in lift coefficient, compared with $+0.0593$ on I, while its control loses only $-0.0079$ rather than $-0.0351$. The resulting total lift increase is larger on II, $+0.0935$ versus $+0.0242$, but its pitching-moment increase is smaller, $+0.0168$ versus $+0.0339$. Each planform retains its own reference area and computed aerodynamic center. The chordwise loads locate the lift difference: the forward region gains more lift on II, the intermediate region gains $+0.0063$ instead of losing $-0.0097$, and the aft loss is reduced from $-0.0332$ on I to $-0.0065$ on II. The smaller intermediate and aft moment increments on II, with similar forward-region contributions, accompany this redistribution.

The pitching response depends additionally on where the aft force changes occur relative to the moment axis. On I, comparable aft lift losses occur across the span, yet the positive moment increments increase toward the tip. On II, aft lift loss and positive moment change are concentrated inboard, while the outboard aft region gains lift. The aft regions lie downstream of the respective numerical aerodynamic centers, but the streamwise arm $x-x_A$ increases outboard on I and decreases on II. Consequently, for an equal loss of upward force, the $-(x-x_A)dF_z$ term in Eq.~\ref{eq:moment} gives a larger positive moment contribution outboard on I. These different spanwise force changes and moment arms explain why the larger total lift increase on II does not produce a larger pitching-moment increase. McCormack and Cook~\cite{mccormack1949sweptforward} documented a related role of inboard chordwise load location on a 45$\degc$ forward-swept wing without controls. In its moderate-lift range, failed aft pressure recovery over the inboard sections shifted their centers of pressure rearward with little change in section lift, spread outboard with incidence, and changed the wing pitching moment before the lift-curve slope decreased. That stall study differs in section, Reynolds number, and control configuration, but it similarly shows that the chordwise and spanwise location of inboard load changes can govern the forward-swept pitching response.

Actual lift growth can still fall short of its preceding trend. The fixed/control lift departures are $-0.0263/-0.0104$ on II, compared with $-0.0692/-0.0414$ on I; even II's positive intermediate-region increment represents reduced growth relative to that trend. Moment departures about the computed aerodynamic centers reinforce each other, with the control contribution larger on I and the two contributions similar on II. At II's original numerical origin, however, the fixed/control departures are $-0.0023/+0.0035$ and partly cancel. The following shear and pressure distributions locate the control-surface flow changes accompanying these integrated differences.

Figure~\ref{fig:reversal_planform} places the increase in II's reverse-flow area fraction beside its loading analysis. The upper-control fraction grows from about 19\% to 47\% and 59\% at $\alpha=0,2,4\degc$. The surface maps in Fig.~\ref{fig:flow_planform} show where this change occurs: at $\alpha=0\degc$, reverse flow near the trailing edge is concentrated around midspan; with increasing incidence, it extends upstream along the control chord and toward the root. The pressure sections across $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ show a weakened upper-side hinge-suction peak inboard and comparatively little change at the outboard cut, consistent with the concentration of aft lift loss inboard.

At $\alpha=2\degc$, II's reverse-flow area fraction is close to I's 49\%, despite its much smaller control lift loss across $\alpha=0\to2\degc$. The differing pressure distributions and load locations explain why comparable reverse-flow areas can accompany different lift and moment changes. \section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The wind-tunnel measurements show that reductions in pre-stall lift growth and changes in pitching moment do not necessarily occur over the same incidence interval or at the same control deflection. Resolving the computed forces into fixed- and control-surface contributions, then examining their spatial distribution, identifies how different combinations of loading changes produce the numerical lift and pitching responses.

The reference computation shows that continued total lift growth can conceal reduced growth on both the fixed surface and the full-span control. For configuration I at zero dihedral and $\delta=20\degc$, fixed lift gain exceeds actual control lift loss, yet the fixed surface accounts for approximately 63\% of the total departure below the preceding trend. The control contributes more strongly to the positive pitching-moment departure about the numerical aerodynamic center.

Pressure sections and regional integrations explain why stronger leading-edge suction is consistent with reduced fixed-surface growth: the forward-region gain falls below its preceding trend, while the intermediate region actually loses lift. Similar aft lift losses produce larger positive moment increments outboard as their downstream moment arms grow. The upper-control shear pattern shifts from reverse flow adjacent to the hinge to reverse flow near the trailing edge across the computed interval. Its subsequent upstream extension coexists with renewed total lift growth. Consequently, the extent of reverse flow complements the pressure and load evidence but does not determine the integrated response by itself.

The measurements establish distinct lift and moment responses across deflection and geometry. RANS reproduces the principal reference trends with a displaced lift interval and substantial coefficient-level bias, while correspondence is weaker for the dihedral and forward-swept cases. The detailed loading findings therefore characterize the computed flow fields and loads.

For matched dihedral, adjacent lift increments reverse their ordering between the two orientations. The common-axis moment comparison also changes from reinforcement to compensation between the contributions from $F_x$ and $F_z$ across incidence intervals. Both the force magnitudes and their moment arms enter this incidence-dependent response.

The forward-swept planform gives stronger fixed-surface lift growth, much weaker control loss, positive intermediate-chord growth and an inboard-concentrated aft lift loss over the matched $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ interval. Consequently, its aft moment increments lack the reference model's increase in positive moment contribution toward the tip about the corresponding aerodynamic centers. This connects the different pitching responses to the chordwise and spanwise organization of the computed loading.

Future computations on the finer levels of the established mesh hierarchy are planned to assess the resolution sensitivity of the pre-stall load redistribution, with particular attention to pitching moment and the fixed- and control-surface contributions. The present joint analysis identifies how continued lift growth can coexist with different component lift deficits and pitching-moment contributions.
