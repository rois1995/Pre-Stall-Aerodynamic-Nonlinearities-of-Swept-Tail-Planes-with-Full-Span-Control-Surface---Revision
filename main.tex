\begin{abstract}
\label{anchor:abstract}
On swept wings with deflected trailing-edge controls, pitching moment can change while total lift continues to grow before stall. Wind-tunnel measurements from MONNALISA show where these changes occur. Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) computations show how the loads on the fixed surface and control contribute. In the reference aft-swept computation, the fixed surface continues to gain lift, but less than predicted by its earlier trend, and accounts for about two-thirds of the total shortfall. The control contributes more to the pitching-moment departure from the earlier trend about the computed aerodynamic center. Stronger suction near the leading edge accompanies a loss of lift farther aft, and changes in the spanwise location of the force affect their moment arms. The model with dihedral has lower lift but gains more lift over part of the incidence range. The forward-swept model loses less lift over the aft chord, and that loss is concentrated inboard. These comparisons show that pitching moment depends on how much the loads change and how those changes are distributed over the wing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Deflecting a trailing-edge control changes both total lift and the lift distribution over the wing. Changes in force at different distances from the reference axis contribute differently to pitching moment. Lift can therefore decrease in one region and change the pitching moment while total lift continues to grow. To explain this pre-stall behavior, we examine the loads on the fixed surface and the control separately.

Plain-flap experiments by Sears and Liddell~\cite{sears1942plain} documented reduced flap effectiveness associated with separation at large deflections. They also showed that similar lift changes can accompany different hinge-moment behavior. On finite rectangular wings with full-span flaps, Campbell et al.~\cite{campbell1956fullspan} related flap chord and aspect ratio to lift and center of pressure. Together, these studies show why the lift distribution matters when interpreting pitching moment.

On swept wings, spanwise changes in local lift can produce pitching-moment nonlinearity before maximum lift~\cite{harper1964sweptstall}. The onset of local stall need not coincide with the change in integrated moment: the force change and its moment arm vary along the span. This motivates analyzing load redistribution instead of assigning a single global stall event.

Swept-flap experiments reinforce this distinction. Tewes et al.~\cite{tewes2014sweepcontrol} found that large deflection could produce separated flap flow with continued lift growth, while sweep altered the associated lift and pitching-moment response. Melton~\cite{melton2014sweptflap} observed spanwise variation in separated flow on a swept wing with a 30\%-chord flap. These lower-Reynolds-number experiments primarily investigated active separation control and cannot quantitatively validate the present configurations. They establish context for the three-dimensional response but do not resolve the fixed- and control-surface contributions.

Reduced-order methods provide another perspective: Sivells and Neely~\cite{sivells1947liftingline} incorporated nonlinear sectional lift and moment data into lifting-line analysis, while Goitia and Llamas~\cite{goitia2019vlm} combined viscous sectional data with vortex-lattice and finite-wing corrections. Such formulations can predict nonlinear loading and pitching moment, while the present RANS solutions resolve the surface pressure and wall shear used to examine where those loads change.

The MONNALISA database study~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa} documented pre-stall lift nonlinearity, reverse flow on the control, associated pressure changes, and continued total lift growth. Its DUST computations~\cite{tugnoli2021dust} used nonlinear sectional data, while the potential-based calculations were smoother. The present study builds on those observations but addresses a different question: how the loads on the fixed surface and control combine to produce changes in lift and pitching moment.

We first compare the measured lift and pitching moment with the RANS results to see which changes the computations reproduce. We then use pressure and skin friction to interpret the computed loads. Comparing the two dihedral settings and the aft- and forward-swept models shows how changes in local force and moment arm affect the integrated response.

The models are isolated tailplane-inspired wings with full-span, 30\%-chord plain controls, without fuselage or wing wake. The results do not establish behavior for partial-span controls or untested geometries.

\label{end:sec:intro}

Model I has $+30\degc$ quarter-chord sweep and model II has $-15\degc$. Positive dihedral raises the tip, and the full-wing aspect ratio is $\Lambda=2b^2/S$.

\subsection{Conventions and moment references}\label{sec:references}
Positive incidence raises the leading edge, positive control deflection moves the trailing edge downward, and positive pitching moment is nose-up. Body axes are downstream ($x$), spanwise ($y$), and upward ($z$). Forces are normalized by $q_\infty S$; lift and drag follow from the body-axis components:
\begin{equation}
 C_L=C_{F,z}\cos\alpha-C_{F,x}\sin\alpha,\qquad
 C_D=C_{F,z}\sin\alpha+C_{F,x}\cos\alpha,\qquad
 C_{F,k}=F_k/(q_\infty S).
\label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
Pitching moment is normalized by $q_\infty S\bar c$. Body coordinates are expressed relative to the root leading edge.

\label{anchor:fitted_reference}We obtained separate wind-tunnel and RANS aerodynamic centers by fitting pitching moment and body-normal force at zero control deflection over $|\alpha|\leq10\degc$~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa}. Each center remains fixed as control deflection changes. Measured and computed positions are 0.6430 and 0.6420~m at zero dihedral and 0.6455 and 0.6435~m at $45\degc$ for model I. They differ by about 1 and 2~mm, respectively. For model II, the positions are $-0.0492$ and $-0.00362$~m, a difference of 45.6~mm. The WT--RANS curves use the respective fitted references.

The original numerical reference is $O=(0.228571,0,0)$~m, at the root quarter-chord of model I. The coefficient at a new streamwise position $x_1$ follows from its value at $x_0$ and the body-normal force
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_1)=C_M(x_0)+\frac{x_1-x_0}{\bar c}C_{F,z}.
\label{eq:transport}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:geometry} lists the computed aerodynamic centers and the area and chord used to normalize the coefficients. To compare computed moment changes at the two dihedral settings about one reference, we evaluate both at $A_I=(0.642000,0,0)$~m, the computed center of the zero-dihedral model. We denote the resulting moment coefficient by $C_M^{A_I}$.

\label{end:sec:references}
\subsection{Numerical setup}\label{sec:numerics}
SU2~\cite{palacios2013stanford} solves compressible ideal-gas RANS with Sutherland viscosity, Spalart--Allmaras turbulence~\cite{spalart1992sa}, and the Bas--Cakmakcioglu transition model~\cite{cakmakcioglu2020bcm}. The mean-flow equations use vertex-centered median-dual cells, Roe fluxes and second-order MUSCL reconstruction~\cite{van1979towards}, Green--Gauss gradients and a Venkatakrishnan limiter~\cite{venkatakrishnan1993accuracy} (coefficient 0.05). The turbulence equation uses first-order upwinding for convection.

\label{anchor:wall_model}The wing has no-slip, adiabatic surfaces. The far field uses freestream conditions, and the root uses a symmetry boundary. The steady calculation uses implicit Euler pseudo-time stepping with a flexible generalized minimal residual (FGMRES) solver and an adaptive Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) number. The convergence check uses a Cauchy criterion on lift and drag over 100 samples, with a typical tolerance of $3\times10^{-6}$.

Hex-dominant, octree-based meshes~\cite{rausa2026monnalisa} resolve the leading edge, hinge junction, trailing edge, and incidence-dependent wake. Boundary-layer extrusion has nominal first-cell height $2\times10^{-6}$~m and growth ratio 1.15. Representative median wall distance is $y^+=0.19$--0.26, with maxima 1.1--1.3.

A four-level grid study on configuration I used $\delta=35\degc$, $\Gamma=0,45\degc$ and $\alpha=0,18\degc$ (Table~\ref{tab:mesh}, Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh}). Nominal meshes had 9.7--12.7 million points versus 50.1--65.9 million on the finest level. Moments in Fig.~\ref{fig:mesh} use the original numerical pole.

Nominal-to-finest differences were 1.20--2.14\% in lift and 1.13--2.49\% in drag. At $\Gamma=0\degc$, $\alpha=18\degc$, $C_M^O$ changed from $-0.813$ to $-0.827$; the $45\degc$ moment sequence was nonmonotonic. We used the nominal mesh to keep the cost of the full case set manageable. The refinement results describe differences in the integrated coefficients for the cases tested. Local component-load errors remain unquantified.

\subsection{Load decomposition and trend departures}\label{sec:analysis}
An increment is the change in a coefficient between two sampled angles of attack. A departure is the increment minus the change predicted by extending the fitted trend at earlier angles. For example, from $\alpha=0\degc$ to $2\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$, the computed fixed-surface lift coefficient rises by 0.0593. Its earlier trend predicts a rise of 0.1285, so the departure is $-0.0692$. The negative sign indicates a smaller increase than predicted by the trend.

We integrate pressure and viscous loads over fixed, control, and tip surfaces using consistent reference quantities. The control includes the hinge junction and blunt trailing edge; tip loads remain in total coefficients.

For lift or moment coefficient $Q$, the actual increment and departure of component $j$ are
\begin{equation}
 \Delta C_{Q,j}=C_{Q,j}(\alpha_b)-C_{Q,j}(\alpha_a),\qquad
 D_{Q,j}=\Delta C_{Q,j}-m_{Q,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}(\alpha_b-\alpha_a).
\label{eq:departure}
\end{equation}
We fit each earlier slope by unweighted least squares. The same incidence samples are used for each component of a given comparison, so component departures sum to the total. For the reference RANS model, the primary fits use $\alpha\in\{-6,-4,-2\}\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$ and $\{-16,-14,-12\}\degc$ for the early change at $\delta=30\degc$. The wind-tunnel comparisons use earlier samples appropriate to their own intervals. These slopes summarize the data before each interval without assuming a flow state. Using other fitting ranges shows how much the departure depends on that choice.

The departure $D=\sum_i w_i C_i$ depends on both the interval endpoints and the samples used to fit the earlier trend. We carry the supplied uncertainty magnitudes from all these samples into two estimates
\begin{equation}
 u_{\mathrm{ind}}=\left[\sum_i(w_i u_i)^2\right]^{1/2},\qquad
 u_{\mathrm{L1}}=\sum_i|w_i|u_i.
\label{eq:uncertainty}
\end{equation}
The first estimate assumes that the errors at successive angles are independent. The second bounds the departure change if each sample error stays within its supplied magnitude. The available uncertainties do not specify correlations between angles or a confidence level for the departure.

Pressure and wall-shear forces are integrated on the triangulated surface over chordwise and spanwise regions. The pressure coefficient is $C_p=(p-p_\infty)/q_\infty$ and wall-shear coefficient $\mathbf C_f=\boldsymbol\tau_w/q_\infty$. The moment about reference abscissa $x_A$ is
\begin{equation}
 C_M(x_A)=\frac{1}{q_\infty S\bar c}
 \int\big[z\,dF_x-(x-x_A)dF_z\big].
\label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
We retain the moments of the axial force, $z\,dF_x$, and the upward force, $-(x-x_A)dF_z$, separately for the dihedral comparison about a common axis. The integrated surface forces and moments agree with the solver totals.

Chordwise regions are forward ($\xi<0.4$), intermediate ($0.4\leq\xi<0.7$), and aft ($\xi\geq0.7$), with $\xi$ the local chord fraction after straightening the control about its hinge. The aft region is further divided into spanwise thirds, using $\eta=s/b$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}). Integrals cover both physical sides, with hinge junction and end-face loads included in the appropriate totals. Pressure cuts at $\eta=0.2,0.5,0.8$ follow the actual surface intersections without smoothing or bridging gaps; the reported regional coefficients are integrated loads, not densities.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.72\textwidth]{figures_revision_load_regions.pdf}
\caption{Reference planform: chordwise loading regions, spanwise thirds, hinge, and pressure-section locations.}\label{fig:regions}\end{figure}

\label{anchor:reversal_definition}On each surface face, we project the downstream chord direction $\mathbf d_c$ onto the face and normalize it to obtain a unit tangent. On the control, $\mathbf d_c$ follows the deflected chord. For a face with unit normal $\mathbf n$, the tangent is $\mathbf t_c=[\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n]/\|\mathbf d_c-(\mathbf d_c\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n\|$. The coefficient $C_{f,c}=\mathbf C_f\cdot\mathbf t_c$ is the wall shear along that tangent.

The reverse-flow area fraction $f_{\mathrm{rev}}$ is the fraction of the upper control surface ($\xi>0.7$, excluding end faces) where $C_{f,c}<0$. A value of 0.5 means that the chordwise wall shear points upstream over half of that surface. The sign identifies the chordwise direction of flow near the wall. Identifying the full three-dimensional separation pattern requires more information about the surface flow~\cite{tobak1982topology,mccormack1949sweptforward}.

\subsection{Pressure distribution and pitching moment}\label{sec:pressure}
Figure~\ref{fig:cp} compares the pressure distributions on both sides of the reference wing at three span stations for $\alpha=0\degc$ and $2\degc$. Leading-edge suction strengthens, and the upper-surface pressure minimum near the hinge becomes less negative. Pressure recovery here is the rise in pressure downstream of that minimum. A stronger suction peak can coexist with a loss of lift elsewhere on the chord, so we integrate the pressure and shear over each chordwise region.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_pressure_sections.pdf}
\caption{Chordwise pressure distributions at three span locations across the reference pre-stall loading change.}\label{fig:cp}\end{figure}

Summed over the semispan, the forward region ($\xi<0.4$) gains 0.0671 in lift coefficient. The earlier trend predicts a gain of 0.1027, giving a departure of $-0.0357$ using the unrounded values (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}a). In the intermediate region, the lift coefficient falls by 0.0097, compared with a predicted rise of 0.0257. Its departure is $-0.0354$.

The aft-region lift coefficient falls by 0.0332, where the previous trend predicts a gain of 0.0063, giving a departure of $-0.0396$. Pressure forces account for nearly all of the lift-growth shortfall. The totals include the viscous contribution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_spatial_changes.pdf}
\caption{Chordwise and spanwise contributions to reference lift and pitching-moment changes over the selected pre-stall interval.}\label{fig:spatial}\end{figure}

The spanwise breakdown uses the actual changes between the two angles. Across the inboard, middle, and outboard thirds of the aft region, lift-coefficient changes are similar ($-0.0103$, $-0.0118$, and $-0.0111$), while the positive moment increments grow toward the tip ($+0.00252$, $+0.00612$, and $+0.00910$, Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}b,c). The aft-swept planform places the outboard loads farther behind the computed aerodynamic center. A reduction in upward force behind that axis produces a nose-up moment change, and the same force reduction produces a larger change farther behind the axis. This is the $-(x-x_A)dF_z$ contribution in Eq.~\ref{eq:moment}. The axial-force term $z\,dF_x$ is small in this case.

At $\delta=20\degc$, this fraction rises from 0.146 to 0.485 across $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, coinciding with the computed lift-growth shortfall and positive moment change. Figure~\ref{fig:transition} shows that the reversed-shear region also changes location.

At zero incidence, the reverse flow near the wall lies chiefly near the hinge. At $\alpha=2\degc$ it is concentrated toward the trailing edge, then extends upstream by $\alpha=4\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:transition}). As the region shifts toward the trailing edge, suction near the hinge weakens, and the aft region loses lift.

By $\alpha=4\degc$, about 59\% of the upper control surface has upstream-directed chordwise shear. Even as this area grows, the model gains more lift from $\alpha=2\degc$ to $4\degc$ (0.0968 in lift coefficient) than from $0\degc$ to $2\degc$ (0.0242).

\subsection{Effect of dihedral}\label{sec:dihedral}
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_dihedral} compares the measurements at the two dihedral settings and the RANS results at $\Gamma=45\degc$. On the 45-degree model at $\delta=20\degc$, measured lift growth stays low from $\alpha=-2\degc$ to $2\degc$. The magnitude of the moment departure from $-2\degc$ to $0\degc$ is below both uncertainty sensitivities in Table~\ref{tab:wt_geometry}. At $\delta=30\degc$, measured lift growth slows from $\alpha=-2\degc$ to $0\degc$, while the moment coefficient falls earlier, by 0.027 from $-4\degc$ to $-2\degc$. The magnitude of that moment departure exceeds the sensitivity based on independent errors and lies within the absolute-weight bound.

Agreement is weaker for the dihedral cases. At $\delta=20\degc$, RANS predicts a lift decrease from $\alpha=-6\degc$ to $-4\degc$ that is absent in the measurements. In the computation, the area of reverse flow on the control increases markedly over those two degrees. Near the measured reduction in lift growth, the computed lift coefficient rises by 0.0763 over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$ and by 0.0444 over the next two degrees. The measured moment change near zero incidence is small relative to uncertainty, while RANS gives a different moment response. At $\delta=30\degc$, the computed moment falls one sampled interval later than measured. We use the calculations below to distinguish how dihedral changes the lift increments and which forces contribute to the computed pitching moment.

At zero incidence and $\delta=20\degc$, the computed lift coefficient is 0.301 lower with $45\degc$ dihedral. Tilting the reference panel-normal force by $45\degc$ at fixed magnitude and projecting it onto the common vertical lift direction accounts algebraically for about 0.247 of this difference.

Over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, the lift coefficient rises by 0.1210 at zero dihedral and by 0.0763 at $45\degc$. Over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, it rises by 0.0242 and 0.0444, respectively. The model with dihedral therefore gains less lift in the first interval and more in the second, even though its lift stays lower. Over the second interval, the 45-degree model gains lift on the fixed surface and loses lift on the control. All three surface groups contribute to the decrease in its pitching-moment coefficient about its own computed aerodynamic center (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget}).

Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget} shows where the changes in pitching moment occur on each model, about its own computed aerodynamic center. Figure~\ref{fig:dihedral} instead uses the same axis $A_I$ for both models so that the difference can be analyzed without changing the reference~\cite{maggin1946dihedral}. Dihedral changes the height of the loads above the axis and hence the moment of the axial force, $z\,dF_x$. The upward force contributes through its streamwise arm, $-(x-x_A)dF_z$. For the same incidence interval, we subtract the zero-dihedral moment increment from the 45-degree increment
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I_M=[C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,45}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
             -[C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_b)-C_{M,0}^{A_I}(\alpha_a)]
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
When $\mathcal I_M$ is negative, the pitching-moment coefficient increases less or decreases more at $45\degc$ than at zero dihedral over the same interval.

At $\delta=20\degc$, the $F_x$ and $F_z$ contributions to the common-axis difference partly cancel over $\alpha=-4\to-2\degc$ ($-0.00362$ and $+0.00131$). Over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$, both are negative ($-0.01283$ and $-0.02750$), giving a total difference of $-0.04033$. The contribution from the upward force is larger in this interval. It depends on how the upward force changes across the surface and on the streamwise distance of each region from the reference axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:dihedral}).

At $\delta=30\degc$, the two terms also reinforce over $\alpha=-2\to0\degc$, giving a common-axis moment-increment difference of $-0.01836$.

Over those same two degrees, the moment coefficient on the $45\degc$ model falls by 0.0148 about its own computed aerodynamic center. The fixed surface contributes $-0.0112$ of that change (Table~\ref{tab:geometry_budget}). The area fraction with reverse flow on the control changes only from 0.889 to 0.911 and is 0.912 at $\alpha=2\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal_geometry}). Since the fixed surface supplies most of the moment change while the reverse-flow area on the control changes little, we examine the fixed-surface pressure and wall shear over the same sequence.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.90\textwidth]{figures_revision_reversal_geometry.pdf}
\caption{Reverse-flow area fractions on the upper control surface for $\Gamma=45\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$ and $\delta=30\degc$.}\label{fig:reversal_geometry}\end{figure}

From $\alpha=-2\degc$ to $0\degc$, chordwise skin friction increases over the fixed surface from midspan toward the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow_dihedral}). At $\alpha=2\degc$, upstream-directed shear appears near the outboard leading edge. The chordwise cut shows that shear points downstream again farther aft and rises sharply. Together with the localized suction peaks, this pattern is consistent with local separation followed by reattachment. The shear rise suggests a change in the boundary layer, although the surface data leave the transition location uncertain. Toppings and Yarusevych~\cite{toppings2021wingtip} observed spanwise changes in separation bubbles on an unswept wing at a different Reynolds number.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_flow_dihedral.pdf}
\caption{$\Gamma=45\degc$, $\delta=30\degc$: upper-surface shear maps (top), upper-surface shear cuts (middle), and pressure sections (bottom) at $\alpha=-2,0,2\degc$.}\label{fig:flow_dihedral}\end{figure}

At zero incidence, the pressure cuts show stronger leading-edge and hinge suction at midspan and outboard than inboard. As chord decreases toward the tip, the leading-edge radius also decreases, which can concentrate acceleration over a shorter distance. Local inflow and Reynolds number vary along the span as well. Both dihedral settings use the same taper, so this geometry helps explain where along the span the pressure gradients can become steeper.

The control pressure changes little over most of its chord from $\alpha=-2\degc$ to $2\degc$, while the fixed-surface pressure and shear change appreciably. These distributions are consistent with the larger fixed-surface contribution to the computed moment decrease from $-2\degc$ to $0\degc$.

\subsection{Aft- and Forward-Swept Planforms}\label{sec:planform}
Configuration II has $-15\degc$ quarter-chord sweep versus $+30\degc$ for I, with the same semispan and full-span control. Alongside the sweep reversal, taper, chord, and area differ between these complete planforms (Table~\ref{tab:geometry}).

At $\delta=20\degc$, the wind-tunnel lift coefficient of model II rises by 0.044 from $\alpha=0\degc$ to $2\degc$, compared with about 0.092 over each neighboring two-degree interval. The moment coefficient rises by 0.019 over $0\degc$ to $2\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison_planform}). The lift change occurs later than on model I and the accompanying moment rise is smaller. For the primary fit, the moment departure exceeds both uncertainty sensitivities in Table~\ref{tab:wt_geometry}. For some other fits, it falls within the primary absolute-weight bound. At $\delta=30\degc$, model II does not show the broad reduction in lift growth at the same negative incidences as model I.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_comparison_planform.pdf}
\caption{Wind-tunnel planform and forward-swept WT--RANS comparisons. WT bars show propagated pointwise uncertainties; grey bands mark the measured $\delta=20\degc$ interval.}\label{fig:comparison_planform}\end{figure}

For the same $0\degc$ to $2\degc$ interval at $\delta=20\degc$, RANS predicts a lift increase of 0.0935, compared with the measured 0.044. About the experimental aerodynamic center, the moment coefficient increases by 0.0099 in RANS and 0.019 in the tunnel. The computed increase is 0.0168 about the RANS aerodynamic center. We obtain the common-reference value using Eq.~\ref{eq:transport} and $\Delta C_{F,z}=0.0952$.

The computed load distributions explain why model II gains more lift but has a smaller nose-up moment increase than model I over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:planform_loading}). Model II gains more lift on the fixed surface and loses less on the control, giving a total lift increase of 0.0935 versus 0.0242. The intermediate region gains lift on model II and loses lift on model I. Model II also loses much less lift over the aft region. Each model uses its own reference area and chord, and the moments in this comparison are about the respective computed aerodynamic centers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures_revision_planform_loading.pdf}
\caption{Planform comparison over $\alpha=0\to2\degc$ at $\delta=20\degc$. Lift increments (top) and moment increments about each model's computed aerodynamic center (bottom).}\label{fig:planform_loading}\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!tp]\centering\includegraphics[width=.50\textwidth]{figures_revision_reversal_planform.pdf}
\caption{Forward-swept upper control surface reverse-flow area fraction at $\delta=20\degc$; the open diamond marks $\alpha=0\degc$, the start of the interval in the loading analysis.}\label{fig:reversal_planform}\end{figure}

The moments depend on both the spanwise distribution of the loads and their distance behind the reference axis. On model I, similar aft lift losses along the span produce progressively larger nose-up moment increments toward the tip, where the aft loads act farther behind the axis. On model II, the downstream moment arm decreases toward the tip. The aft region loses lift inboard and gains lift outboard, so the nose-up contribution is concentrated inboard. This helps explain why the computed moment increment for model II is about half that of model I despite its larger lift increment. McCormack and Cook~\cite{mccormack1949sweptforward} also linked inboard pressure changes to pitching moment on a forward-swept wing without a control surface.

Although model II gains more lift, both its fixed surface and its control fall short of their earlier trends. Their departures are $-0.0263$ and $-0.0104$, compared with $-0.0692$ and $-0.0414$ on model I. The two moment departures are positive about each model's computed aerodynamic center. About the original numerical origin of model II, the fixed-surface and control departures are $-0.0023$ and $+0.0035$ and partly cancel.

On model II, the upper-control area fraction with reverse flow rises from about 19\% at zero incidence to 47\% at $\alpha=2\degc$ and 59\% at $4\degc$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:reversal_planform}). The maps show the region spreading upstream and toward the root (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow_planform}). From $\alpha=0\degc$ to $2\degc$, suction near the hinge weakens inboard and changes little outboard, consistent with the aft lift loss being concentrated inboard.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The wind-tunnel measurements show that lift growth can slow and pitching moment can change over different ranges of angle of attack. The computed loads show how the fixed surface and control contribute to the numerical response. On the aft-swept model at zero dihedral and $20\degc$ control deflection, total lift still rises from $0\degc$ to $2\degc$ angle of attack. The fixed surface accounts for about 63\% of the shortfall relative to the earlier lift trend. The control contributes more to the pitching-moment departure about the computed aerodynamic center.

In that interval, suction strengthens near the leading edge while lift falls over the intermediate chord. Similar losses of aft lift along the span produce larger nose-up moment increments toward the tip, where the loads act farther behind the reference axis. Reverse flow on the control changes location as these loads change, and its area continues to grow even when total lift growth picks up again.

At $20\degc$ control deflection, the model with $45\degc$ dihedral gains less lift than the zero-dihedral model from $-2\degc$ to $0\degc$ angle of attack, then gains more from $0\degc$ to $2\degc$. About a common axis, the axial and upward forces contribute to the difference in moment changes between the two models. Those contributions partly cancel in some intervals and reinforce in others. From $0\degc$ to $2\degc$ angle of attack, the forward-swept model at zero dihedral gains more lift than the aft-swept model at the same setting, but its nose-up moment increment is smaller. It loses less lift over the aft chord, and that lift loss and the associated nose-up moment change are concentrated inboard.

In the cases examined here, a rising total lift curve can conceal local lift losses that change the pitching moment. Finer grids would help assess how sensitive the computed moment contributions are to mesh resolution.
